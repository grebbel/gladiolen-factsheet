Outdoor **experiment** in the Netherlands testing whether a neonicotinoid insecticide (thiacloprid) **causes** a decline in water insects. Not about gladiolus or bulb fields. The text snapshot has no page numbers; references are to sections.

## Key findings

- **Design:** 36 experimental ditches (10 m long, 1,750 litres each) at Leiden University's outdoor "Living Lab", colonised naturally from an adjoining lake. Thiacloprid was added at 0, 0.1, 1 and 10 µg/L, nine ditches per concentration, twice (mid-May and two weeks later). Emerging insects were trapped from mid-April to mid-July (Materials and Methods).
- 55,574 emerging aquatic insects were collected (Results).
- **Result:** with increasing thiacloprid concentration, all five major insect orders (beetles, flies and midges, mayflies, dragonflies and damselflies, caddisflies) declined strongly in abundance and biomass. At 10 µg/L emergence of most orders was "nearly absent" (Abstract).
- Species diversity of non-biting midges (Chironomidae), the most species-rich family, halved at 1 µg/L and was generally reduced to a single species at 10 µg/L (Abstract; Discussion).
- One midge species increased with concentration, which the authors explain as a predator taking over a niche left by declining dragonflies; total abundance was therefore a misleading measure (Discussion).
- **Authors' conclusion:** the findings "showcase a causal link of neonicotinoids and the ongoing insect decline" (Abstract). They call for reconsidering the "mass usage" of neonicotinoids (Abstract).
- **Field realism (authors):** the 1 µg/L spike averaged 0.3 µg/L over the first month, close to reported geometric means for neonicotinoid peak and average surface-water concentrations (0.63 and 0.13 µg/L, from 27 studies). For the Netherlands, when thiacloprid was detected in surface water in 2013–2017, 8% of samples were in the range 0.1–1 µg/L (Discussion).
- The authors note that neonicotinoid use has also been associated with declines in insect-eating birds, possibly through loss of insect food (Discussion).

## Methods and limitations

- Controlled outdoor experiment with replicates and controls: strong design for **causality**.
- One substance (thiacloprid), one site, one season; no mixtures with other pesticides and no adjuvants.
- Ditches are model ecosystems, not real field ditches next to crops.

## Quality flags

- Peer-reviewed (PNAS), Dutch, recent. **Strong** evidence that thiacloprid at field-realistic concentrations can cause declines in water insects.
- Not gladiolus-specific. Link to gladiolus: thiacloprid was used on gladiolus in 2012–2020 (on 25–31% of the area; [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]]) and was sprayed on bulb fields in the OBO study ([[figueiredo-2021-resident-exposure-protocol]]). No data on thiacloprid concentrations in ditches next to gladiolus fields were found.
- Co-author M.G. Vijver also wrote the methodological rebuttal of a field study on imidacloprid ([[vijver-2014-imidacloprid-rebuttal]]); here she co-authors experimental evidence for a causal effect of a related substance.
