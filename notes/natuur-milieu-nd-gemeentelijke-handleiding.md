Source ID in [[source-overview]]: S9. Written in Dutch. A legal guide for municipalities, compiled by environmental NGOs and law firms, supported by organisations including PAN Netherlands, Greenpeace Nederland, ParkinsonNL and Vogelbescherming Nederland (p. 8).

**Date:** no publication date found. The most recent dates cited are October 2024 (p. 11) and 10 November 2024 (p. 12, note 15), so it was written in or after November 2024. Exact date not found in sources.

**Nature of the source:** an advocacy-oriented legal guide. Its legal analysis (what municipalities may do) is the main value. Its summaries of health science are secondary and selective, and should not be cited as evidence of health effects.

PDF page numbers equal printed page numbers.

## Key findings

**Who regulates what (p. 6):**
- National government (Rijk): rules on use (Besluit activiteiten leefomgeving, Bal; Wet gewasbeschermingsmiddelen en biociden, Wgb; Besluit gewasbeschermingsmiddelen en biociden, Bgb) and on authorisation.
- Ctgb (Board for the Authorisation of Plant Protection Products and Biocides): authorises products and sets conditions of use (Regulation (EC) 1107/2009, Wgb, Bgb).
- Provinces: Natura 2000, nature network, groundwater protection areas, Water Framework Directive (KRW) groundwater targets.
- Water boards: KRW targets for regional waters; rules on discharges.
- Municipalities: balanced allocation of functions to locations; protecting the health of residents and vulnerable groups.

**Legal room for local rules (as argued by the authors):**
- Regulation (EC) 1107/2009, article 1(4), allows member states to apply the precautionary principle where risks are scientifically uncertain (p. 16).
- The Sustainable Use Directive 2009/128/EC, article 12, requires pesticide use to be prohibited or minimised in areas used by the public or vulnerable groups (parks, school grounds, playgrounds, near healthcare facilities), and in Natura 2000 and protected water areas (p. 17).
- The Wgb gives municipalities no basis for their own rules on pesticide use; the Omgevingswet (Environment and Planning Act) does, through the municipal environment plan (*omgevingsplan*) (p. 18).
- Under the Omgevingswet there is no longer an obligation to include standard transitional law protecting existing lawful use, which the authors (citing Koeman) say gives municipalities more room to act on existing agricultural use (p. 18).
- Possible municipal instruments: general rules or permit requirements in the environment plan (buffer zones, crop bans, product bans); custom rules or individual custom requirements (*maatwerkvoorschriften*) under the Bal; conditions in lease contracts and land sales where the municipality owns the land (pp. 7; 21–27; chapters 7–9).
- A total ban on pesticides on all farmland would probably fail the proportionality test (p. 24). Rules must be well-founded and proportionate (pp. 23–24).

**Bulbs and gladiolus:**
- The guide gives a model rule for a "sierteeltvrije zone" (ornamental-crop-free zone) in which bulb and flower growing is not allowed near homes, with an exception for organic (SKAL-certified) growing unless paraffin oil is used (pp. 25–26).
- It recommends basing the width of spray-free zones on the 250 m distance within which RIVM found "duidelijk verhoogde concentraties" around bulb fields (p. 25). See quality flags.
- Examples: Opsterland (ban on new ornamental, fruit and tree growing) and Haaksbergen (ban on new bulb, ornamental, tree and fruit growing in the rural zoning plan) (p. 26).
- Gladiolus is not mentioned. The guide focuses on lily growing as the main concern (p. 8; p. 18).

**Case law cited (p. 4, note 2; p. 18):** courts in Noord-Nederland (12 June 2023), Limburg (8 May 2024), Oost-Brabant (19 July 2024) and the Arnhem-Leeuwarden court of appeal imposed (partial) bans on pesticide use in lily growing. The judgments themselves are not in `sources/`.

## Figures and data

- Arable and horticultural land: 18.6% of the Netherlands (631,000 ha), citing CBS 2022 (p. 4).
- Pesticide use in lily growing: 113.7 kg/ha per year (2020); silage maize: 0.8 kg/ha (p. 18; source given in a footnote not fully extracted).
- 46 active substances listed as candidates for substitution (October 2024), out of 245 approved for use in the Netherlands (10 November 2024) (pp. 11–12).
- RIVM estimate cited: 20% of authorised products contain one or more substances of very high concern (ZZS) and 10% contain a potential ZZS (p. 12).
- 37 EU-approved active substances contain PFAS (p. 12).
- Netherlands second only to Malta in the EU for pesticide use per hectare of agricultural land, citing FAOSTAT (p. 13).
- European Environment Agency: 53% of Dutch surface water contained too many pesticides between 2016 and 2021 (p. 14).

## Methods and limitations

- Legal desk analysis; no new data.
- Health and environment chapter (pp. 13–15) summarises selected studies to support regulation; it is not a systematic review.

## Quality flags

- Advocacy organisations and law firms; purpose is to support regulation. Legal arguments are the authors' interpretation; they have not all been tested in court.
- Not gladiolus-specific.
- No publication date.
- **Characterisation of RIVM differs from the RIVM report itself:** S9 says RIVM found "aanzienlijk verhoogde concentraties" up to at least 250 m around bulb fields, including in house dust, and that residents are "chronisch" exposed via house dust (pp. 13; 15; 25). The RIVM summary ([[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]], p. 24; p. 32) reports average concentrations about 10 times (air) and 5 times (house dust) higher within 250 m than at control homes more than 500 m away, notes that concentrations within 250 m differed little by distance, and found no exceedance of risk limits. When citing, use the RIVM figures, not S9's wording.
- Health claims (Parkinson, cancer, autism) are hazard and association evidence cited by the authors; not assessed here.
