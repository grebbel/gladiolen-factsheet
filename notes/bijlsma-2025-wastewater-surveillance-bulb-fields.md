## Key findings

- The study tested wastewater-based surveillance (WBS): measuring human urinary metabolites of pesticides in untreated sewage, as an anonymous, pooled sample of a whole community (p. 1, abstract and Introduction).
- 71 untreated wastewater samples were taken in May and early June 2020, during the pesticide application period: 31 from flower bulb areas (Tollebeek, Lisse) and 40 from control areas (Utrecht, Nijkerk, Nieuwe Wetering) (p. 1, abstract; p. 2, section 2.2).
- 8 of the 11 metabolites (biomarkers) were not detected in any sample, including those for chlorpropham, asulam, boscalid, pyrethroids, lambda-cyhalothrin, imidacloprid, thiacloprid and acetamiprid (p. 3, section 3; p. 4, section 4).
- Tebuconazole (fungicide): its metabolite loads in Tollebeek (flower bulb area) were significantly higher than in Utrecht (control). It was not detected in Lisse (bulb area) or in the other two control areas (p. 4, section 3).
- S-metolachlor (herbicide): its metabolite was found in both bulb and control areas. Loads in Tollebeek were significantly higher than elsewhere. Lisse was slightly higher than the controls, but not significantly (p. 4, section 3).
- A linuron metabolite (3,4-DCA) was detected below the quantification limit in two samples from Lisse (p. 3, section 3; p. 4).
- The authors conclude that residents near flower bulb fields were exposed to "higher pesticide levels" than people in control areas, and call for wider monitoring (p. 5).
- The authors state that excretion data are lacking, so the results cannot yet be translated into intake or health risk (p. 5, Conclusions). **This is a detection study, not a study of harm.**
- Results partly differ from the earlier Dutch OBO urine study: OBO found the chlorpropham metabolite in 97% of urine samples from bulb-area residents (67% in control areas), but this study found no chlorpropham metabolite in wastewater. For asulam both studies agree (rare or no detection) (p. 4, section 4).
- Some insecticides, such as imidacloprid, are not registered for use on bulb fields but are used for bulb disinfection in (closed) storage. The authors expect airborne exposure of residents from this use to be limited, and workers to be more exposed (p. 4, section 4; p. 2, Table 1 note b).

## Figures and data

- Share of Dutch homes within 250 m of at least one cultivated agricultural field: approximately 27% (p. 2, Introduction).
- OBO study (cited): pesticide concentrations in outdoor air around bulb fields 5–10 times higher during application periods than in reference areas (p. 2, Introduction).
- OBO study (cited): tebuconazole metabolite in 61% of urine samples (n = 99) from residents near bulb fields during the 2017 application period; 43% in control areas (> 500 m) and 24% outside the application period (p. 4, section 4).

## Methods and limitations

- 24-hour composite samples at five wastewater treatment plant inlets; LC-MS/MS analysis; loads normalised by flow and population served; one-way ANOVA (pp. 2–3).
- Limitations stated by the authors: sampling during the first Covid-19 lockdown, which may have changed exposure (more time at home) and limited sampling days; assumption of uniform pesticide use across bulb regions and uniform diet across cities; detection limit for the terbuthylazine metabolite about 10 times higher than in other studies, so false negatives are possible; no excretion factors for most metabolites (pp. 4–5).
- Non-detection may mean concentrations were too low, the pesticide was not applied, or application did not coincide with sampling (p. 4).

## Quality flags

- Not gladiolus-specific: flower bulb areas in general.
- Small number of locations (two bulb areas), and the two bulb areas gave different results.
- Single season (spring 2020), during a lockdown.
- Measures detection of metabolites, not harm or health risk.
- Authors used ChatGPT for language editing (declared, p. 5).
