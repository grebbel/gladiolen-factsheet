Phase 3 of the [[source-overview]] workflow, updated after the scope was broadened to related crops in the iris family (Iridaceae). Draws only on the source notes S1–S19. Every finding links to the notes it rests on; page, table or file references are in those notes.

## How to read this

**Evidence strength** (applied per finding):

- **Strong**: several independent sources agree, or official statistics or one large, well-designed study, relevant to the Netherlands and to the question.
- **Moderate**: one good-quality source, or several sources that agree but share limitations (age, crop group, design).
- **Limited**: a single source with clear limitations (old, small, not crop-specific, advocacy or trade source, values read from a chart, or secondary citation).
- **Conflicting**: sources disagree; both are reported.

**Labels and distinctions kept throughout:**

- **[gladiolus]**: the source is specific to gladiolus. **[related crop: …]**: iris, crocus or freesia. No label: flower bulbs or agriculture in general.
- **Open field** and **greenhouse** findings are kept apart. Only freesia is a greenhouse crop here.
- *Hazard* (what a substance can do) is not *risk* (likelihood of harm at actual exposure).
- *Detection* (a substance is found) is not *harm* (an effect is shown).

**Overall picture of the evidence:** official CBS statistics ([[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]], [[cbs-2026-80780ned-areaal-iridaceae]], [[cbs-2026-85430NED-handel-gladiolen]]) now give recent, gladiolus-specific figures on area, pesticide use and trade. Evidence on environmental and health effects remains about flower bulbs in general; none of it separates gladiolus or other Iridaceae.

## 1. Area of gladiolus and related Iridaceae cultivation

**1.1 [gladiolus] The area of gladiolus bulbs fell by about 60% between 2000 and 2026, from about 1,643 ha to 662 ha (provisional).** It was about 1,118 ha in 2010, 812 ha in 2020 and 700 ha in 2025. The number of farms fell from 320 to 90.
Source: [[cbs-2026-80780ned-areaal-iridaceae]]. Consistent with [[cbs-2025-data-exports-fase1]] (CBS news item, 2014–2024).
Strength: **strong** (official annual statistics).

**1.2 [gladiolus] Gladiolus is also grown as an open-field cut flower: about 118–169 ha per year in 2017–2026.**
Source: [[cbs-2026-80780ned-areaal-iridaceae]]. Strength: **strong** (official statistics; available from 2017 only).

**1.3 [gladiolus] Gladiolus is a small and shrinking share of Dutch bulb growing:** about 7% of the total bulb area in 2000 and about 2% in 2026, while the total bulb area grew from about 22,513 to 28,418 ha.
Source: [[cbs-2026-80780ned-areaal-iridaceae]]. Strength: **strong**.

**1.4 [gladiolus] Gladiolus is grown across the country, unlike tulips and hyacinths, which are concentrated in a few regions.** In a recent but unspecified year, the largest share of the gladiolus area was in the Zuidelijk Veehouderijgebied (27%), followed by Veenkoloniën en Oldambt (13%), Westelijk Holland (12%) and the Zuidwestelijk Akkerbouwgebied (12%). Older sources place gladiolus mainly in the south-east and south-west and the IJsselmeerpolders (2004), and in Limburg and Noord-Brabant (2007).
Sources: [[cbs-2025-data-exports-fase1]], [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]], [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]].
Strength: **moderate** (official statistics, but the year of the regional split is not stated; older sources use different regions).

**1.5 [related crops] Iris, crocus and freesia have also declined.** Iris bulbs fell from about 675 ha (2000) to 180 ha (2026); crocus bulbs from about 628 to 314 ha. **Freesia (greenhouse)** fell from about 221 ha (2000) to 50 ha (2025).
Source: [[cbs-2026-80780ned-areaal-iridaceae]]. Strength: **strong**.

**1.6 Different area statistics give different figures, for known reasons.** CBS (agricultural census) gives higher gladiolus areas than the bulb inspection service BKD: for 2000, about 1,643 ha (CBS) against 1,339 ha (BKD). S7 explains that CBS includes headlands and spray-free zones and attributes area to the farm's postal address.
Sources: [[cbs-2026-80780ned-areaal-iridaceae]], [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]], [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]].
Strength: **moderate**. Different measures, not an error.

**1.7 [gladiolus] The 1995 area remains uncertain.** S5 gives 903 ha for 1995 (BKD); the S7 chart (also BKD) shows about 1,900 ha. S5 also shows an unexplained doubling between 1997 and 1998.
Sources: [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]], [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]]. Strength: **conflicting**. Historical only; CBS data start in 2000.

## 2. Imports of gladiolus and related crops

**2.1 [gladiolus] The Netherlands imports few gladiolus corms and exports many.** In 2015–2025, imports were 2–33 million corms per year, mainly from outside the EU; exports were 464–755 million per year, worth €26–38 million.
Source: [[cbs-2026-85430NED-handel-gladiolen]].
Strength: **strong** for the order of magnitude (official trade statistics; 2021 missing; recent years provisional; transit trade included).

**2.2 [gladiolus] Imports of cut gladioli are small: 0–15 million stems per year, almost all from other EU countries.** Exports were 23–105 million stems per year.
Source: [[cbs-2026-85430NED-handel-gladiolen]].
Strength: **strong** for imports. The export values for 2025 and early 2026 imply a much higher price per stem than earlier years and need checking.

**2.3 Countries of origin within "EU" and "non-EU": not found in sources.**

**2.4 [related crops] Trade in iris, crocus and freesia: not found in sources** (no separate customs codes in the CBS table).

**2.5 Context: the Netherlands is a major hub for the flower trade.** S8 states that more than 60% of world trade in flowers and plants passes through Dutch auctions.
Source: [[mantingh-2026-pesticiden-in-boeketten]]. Strength: **limited** (NGO citing a trade website).

## 3. Pesticides used on gladiolus and related crops

### Gladiolus, open field

**3.1 [gladiolus] Pesticide use per hectare on gladiolus fell by about 80% between 2012 and 2024: from 41.9 kg active substance per ha (2012) to 35.7 (2016), 19.4 (2020) and 8.2 (2024, provisional).** Total use fell from 46,675 to 5,756 kg. Pesticides were used on 99.6–100% of the gladiolus area in all four years.
Source: [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]]. Consistent with [[cbs-2025-data-exports-fase1]].
Strength: **strong** (official statistics; but see 3.4 on soil disinfection).

**3.2 [gladiolus] Fungicides made up most of the use and fell the most:** 30.4 kg/ha in 2012, 3.6 kg/ha in 2024. Herbicides fell from 8.8 to 3.9 kg/ha and insecticides from 2.6 to 0.4 kg/ha. In 2024, herbicides slightly exceeded fungicides by weight.
Source: [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]]. Strength: **strong**.

**3.3 [gladiolus] The substances used have changed.** In 2012 the largest by weight was mancozeb (14,858 kg, on 68% of the area), followed by fluazinam, glyphosate, captan and metamitron. Mancozeb was banned in 2021. In 2024 the largest were folpet, pendimethalin, metamitron, glyphosate and captan. The number of different substances recorded rose from 23 (2012) to 34 (2024).
Sources: [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]], [[pan-nl-2025-analyse-cbs-cijfers]].
Strength: **strong** for which substances were used and how much. Less weight does not mean lower hazard; the statistics do not measure toxicity.

**3.4 [gladiolus] Soil disinfection is probably not included in the CBS figures, but is used before planting gladiolus.** CBS records no use of metam-sodium on any crop, although it is in its substance list. S7 reports that soil was disinfected before gladiolus planting against nematodes, and that this increased after 2005, "mainly" in gladiolus growing in Limburg and Noord-Brabant; in 2007 the bulb sector used on average 50 kg/ha of metam-sodium on 3,457 ha.
Sources: [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]], [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]].
Strength: **limited** (CBS coverage not stated by CBS; soil disinfection data from one sector report, 2007). Current extent of soil disinfection for gladiolus: not found in sources.

**3.5 [gladiolus] Spraying targets mainly the fungal disease "vuur" (*Botrytis gladioli*) and thrips, usually weekly, from about June into November** (around 2005).
Source: [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]]. Strength: **limited** (single source).

**3.6 [gladiolus] Historical use: about 27 kg/ha in 1995, most of it fungicides** (values read from a chart). This figure comes from a different survey and method than the CBS series and should not be read as a trend with 3.1.
Source: [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]]. Strength: **limited**.

**3.7 [gladiolus] Compared with other bulb crops, gladiolus is mid-range.** In 2020 use per hectare was 113.7 kg/ha for lily bulbs, 25.8 for tulips, 19.4 for gladiolus, 17.0 for hyacinths and 17.0 for narcissus, against 7.0 for all crops.
Source: [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]]. Strength: **strong**.

**3.8 [gladiolus] Spraying adapted to the crop could reduce use by about 35% over a season, according to a model** based on one trial field in 2002.
Source: [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]]. Strength: **limited** (not tested in practice).

### Related crops

**3.9 [related crop: iris] Pesticide use on irises was about 27–35 kg/ha in 1995–2000** (read from a chart). Current use on iris and crocus: not found in sources (not separate in CBS).
Source: [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]]. Strength: **limited**.

**3.10 [related crop: freesia, greenhouse] Thrips is hard to control in freesia; the range of chemical products is narrow, with a high risk of resistance.** In one commercial greenhouse in 2009, biological control failed and broad-spectrum insecticides were used (spinosad, deltamethrin, methiocarb, thiamethoxam).
Source: [[van-der-staaij-2010-trips-freesia]]. Strength: **limited** (one case, 2009). Current use on freesia: not found in sources.

### Flower bulbs in general

**3.11 The bulb sector is among the most intensive pesticide users.** Use per hectare in bulbs was the highest of all sectors after mushrooms (1995–2000), and 102 kg/ha including soil disinfection in 2007.
Sources: [[molema-nd-gewasafhankelijk-toedienen-gladiolen]], [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]].
Strength: **moderate** (old). Not comparable with the CBS crop figures, which probably exclude soil disinfection.

**3.12 Several bulb-related substances have been withdrawn:** carbendazim (for bulbs, from 1 March 2003), aldicarb (1 July 2007), pirimiphos-methyl (for bulbs, from April 2014), mancozeb (2021), thiophanate-methyl (2023) and oxamyl (October 2023).
Sources: [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]], [[stowa-2022-erfafspoeling-spuitdrift]], [[pan-nl-2025-analyse-cbs-cijfers]].
Strength: **moderate**.

**3.13 National use (all crops) fell by almost 22% between 2020 and 2024**, mainly because of the mancozeb ban and less paraffin oil. PAN-NL argues that more toxic, low-dose insecticides and PFAS pesticides were used on a larger area; this is a hazard argument.
Source: [[pan-nl-2025-analyse-cbs-cijfers]]. Strength: **limited** (NGO reanalysis; the gladiolus figures in 3.1 come directly from CBS).

**3.14 Residues on cut gladioli: not found in sources.** The one residue survey (17 bouquets, 2026) sampled no gladioli.
Source: [[mantingh-2026-pesticiden-in-boeketten]].

**Gaps:** current authorised products for gladiolus, iris, crocus and freesia (Ctgb); current soil and corm disinfection practice for gladiolus; current use on iris, crocus and freesia.

## 4. Environmental effects

**4.1 In surface water in bulb areas, 13–19% of samples exceeded the maximum permissible risk level (MTR) for four substances of concern in 2001–2007; after improvement since 1995, progress had stalled.** The substances were carbendazim, aldicarb sulfoxide, pirimiphos-methyl and imidacloprid.
Source: [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]].
Strength: **moderate** (water-board monitoring; reported by a sector body; data up to 2007). An MTR exceedance is a risk indicator, not observed ecological harm.

**4.2 Which emission route matters most is disputed.**

- *Farmyard first:* S7 (2007 data), S18 (expert assessment, 2012) and S17 (trade article, 2020) identify the farmyard as the main source in bulb growing: run-off after bulb disinfection and crate cleaning, and condensate from storage.
- *Drift first:* S19 (WUR, CLM and RIVM, 2022, based mainly on bulb farms sampled in 2016–2017) concludes that spray drift is probably the most important factor in exceedances overall. Farmyard emissions dominate only for substances used solely as bulb dips, where they can cause high peaks (estimated 10–100 µg/l in the farmyard ditch).
Sources: [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]], [[de-werd-2012-emissieroutes-water-abc]], [[bisschops-2020-flow-schuimen-erfemissie]], [[stowa-2022-erfafspoeling-spuitdrift]].
Strength: **conflicting**. Part of the difference may reflect improved farmyard practice: S19 reports 68–75% lower concentrations in farmyard pits after growers took measures. S19 is the most recent and multi-institutional source.

**4.3 For several substances used in bulb growing, one spray can give ditch concentrations above water-quality standards, according to standard drift calculations.** Examples: pendimethalin about 1.52 µg/l against a standard of 0.018 µg/l; imidacloprid 0.27 against 0.0083; S-metolachlor 1.37 against 0.4. Pendimethalin was the second-largest substance by weight on gladiolus in 2024 (882 kg, on 90% of the area).
Sources: [[stowa-2022-erfafspoeling-spuitdrift]], [[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]].
Strength: **moderate** (calculated, not measured in ditches; standard assumptions). Not gladiolus-specific; the link to gladiolus is only that the same substance is used.

**4.4 Carbendazim persisted in the environment after its withdrawal for bulbs, because thiophanate-methyl, used in bulb disinfection, breaks down into it.** It was found in surface water (to 2007) and in almost all indoor dust samples near bulb fields (2016–2017). Thiophanate-methyl was banned in 2023.
Sources: [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]], [[figueiredo-2021-resident-exposure-protocol]], [[pan-nl-2025-analyse-cbs-cijfers]].
Strength: **moderate**.

**4.5 Context: 53% of Dutch surface water contained too many pesticides in 2016–2021** (European Environment Agency, as cited by S9).
Source: [[natuur-milieu-nd-gemeentelijke-handleiding]]. Strength: **limited** (secondary; all agriculture).

**Gaps:** measured effects on soil life, pollinators, birds or aquatic organisms near gladiolus or other Iridaceae; measured water quality near gladiolus fields; greenhouse emissions from freesia (S18 notes greenhouses were handled in a separate project).

## 5. Health effects and exposure

### Exposure (detection)

**5.1 People living near bulb fields take in pesticides from those fields.** In the OBO study (2016–2017), residues were found in outdoor air, doormat and house dust, and in the urine of adults and children nearby. Average concentrations within 250 m were about 10 times higher in outdoor air and 5 times higher in house dust than at homes more than 500 m from fields. A 2020 wastewater study found higher levels of tebuconazole and S-metolachlor breakdown products in one of two bulb areas.
Sources: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]], [[figueiredo-2021-resident-exposure-protocol]], [[bijlsma-2025-wastewater-surveillance-bulb-fields]].
Strength: **strong** for *detection* near bulb fields. Not gladiolus-specific: the crops on the OBO fields are not named in the sources. Some substances measured (for example tebuconazole, pendimethalin) were also used on gladiolus in 2024 ([[cbs-2025-85130NED-gewasbescherming-per-gewas]]).

**5.2 People living far from fields are exposed too;** RIVM states food probably contributes a large part of what was measured in urine.
Source: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]]. Strength: **moderate**.

**5.3 Growers and their families had higher exposure at home:** house dust about 10 times and outdoor air about 2 times higher than at other residents' homes; urine levels did not clearly differ.
Source: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]]. Strength: **limited**.

**5.4 Concentrations vary widely; measured situations were neither best- nor worst-case.** No drift reached homes during fieldwork; in experiments drift was measurable at 50 m and raised exposure more than tenfold.
Source: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]]. Strength: **moderate**.

### Risk and health outcomes

**5.5 For the pesticides studied, measured levels in air and urine did not exceed available risk limits.** RIVM stresses this is not a final judgement: no worst case was measured, only some substances in one crop group were studied, and combined exposure was not assessed.
Source: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]]. Strength: **moderate**.

**5.6 Studies of health outcomes found no association with living near flower bulbs, but their exposure measures were coarse.** A nationwide cohort (exposure 1995–2003) found hazard ratios close to 1 for flower bulbs; a 2018 RIVM health survey found no health problems linked to bulb growing, but RIVM notes its distance categories were probably not able to show differences.
Sources: [[simoes-2022-residential-proximity-mortality]], [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]].
Strength: **limited**. No association found is not proof of no harm.

**5.7 Hazard claims (Parkinson's disease, cancer, autism and others) appear in the advocacy sources as summaries of other literature** and are not used as findings here.
Sources: [[natuur-milieu-nd-gemeentelijke-handleiding]], [[mantingh-2026-pesticiden-in-boeketten]].

### Workers

**5.8 Bulb disinfection brings workers into contact with disinfectants; methods that avoid wetting crates reduce this contact** (trade article).
Source: [[bisschops-2020-flow-schuimen-erfemissie]]. Strength: **limited**.

**Gaps:** measured exposure and health of gladiolus or freesia growers and workers (S8 only cites BuRO advice on gloves for people handling imported cut flowers); health studies that separate gladiolus; combined exposure; vulnerable groups.

## 6. Regulation and enforcement

**6.1 Pesticides are authorised under EU Regulation (EC) 1107/2009. In the Netherlands the Ctgb authorises products and sets conditions of use. Since early 2014 the Ctgb assesses risks for residents, and since 2016 it uses the EFSA model.**
Sources: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]], [[natuur-milieu-nd-gemeentelijke-handleiding]], [[simoes-2022-residential-proximity-mortality]].
Strength: **strong**.

**6.2 A current list of products authorised for gladiolus, iris, crocus or freesia: not found in sources.** The Ctgb database is organised by product and refuses automated access (see [[source-overview]]).

**6.3 RIVM found the EFSA model does not underestimate exposure for the substances studied, but recommended adding house dust and combined exposure, and national rules on spray distance and drift reduction.** Whether implemented: not found in sources.
Source: [[montforts-2019-bestrijdingsmiddelen-omwonenden]]. Strength: **moderate**.

**6.4 The EU Sustainable Use Directive (2009/128/EC, article 12) requires pesticide use to be banned or minimised in areas used by the public or vulnerable groups.**
Sources: [[natuur-milieu-nd-gemeentelijke-handleiding]], [[simoes-2022-residential-proximity-mortality]]. Strength: **moderate**.

**6.5 How much municipalities can and should do is disputed.** S9 (NGOs and law firms) argues the Omgevingswet allows buffer zones and crop or product bans, and recommends 250 m spray-free zones. The municipality of Berg en Dal (2026) accepts it has these powers but sees regulation as primarily national, has no plans for local rules, and keeps a 50 m distance for new homes based on case law.
Sources: [[natuur-milieu-nd-gemeentelijke-handleiding]], [[berg-en-dal-2026-raadsvragen-bestrijdingsmiddelen]].
Strength: **conflicting**.

**6.6 There are no legal maximum residue limits for pesticides on cut flowers, ornamental plants or flower bulbs.**
Source: [[mantingh-2026-pesticiden-in-boeketten]]. Strength: **limited**.

**6.7 Compliance has been a problem where it was checked.** In 2007 in the bulb sector, reported products did not match those used at 62.5% of firms checked, and water boards found violations at 8–40% of firms. In 2024–2025 only 39% of 71 inspected cut-flower growers under glass complied (NVWA, as cited by S8). Berg en Dal does not check farmers' logbooks; the NVWA is the main supervisor.
Sources: [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]], [[mantingh-2026-pesticiden-in-boeketten]], [[berg-en-dal-2026-raadsvragen-bestrijdingsmiddelen]].
Strength: **limited** (old, secondary or not crop-specific).

**6.8 Sector and government have run farmyard-emission projects** ("Schoon erf, schone sloot" and others); farmyard-pit concentrations fell by 68–75% after measures.
Source: [[stowa-2022-erfafspoeling-spuitdrift]]. Strength: **limited** (personal communication cited in a report).

**Gaps:** NVWA inspection results for bulb or gladiolus growing; the court judgments themselves; current national rules on spray distances.

### Thin

- Environmental and health evidence is for flower bulbs in general; the link to gladiolus rests only on shared substances and practices.
- Historical gladiolus use data (1995) and several figures are read from charts (S5, S7).
- Several sources are advocacy (S8, S9, S10), trade press (S17) or secondary citations (BuRO, NVWA, EEA, court cases). Internal inconsistencies were found in S8 and S10.
- CBS gladiolus use data cover four survey years; 2024 is provisional.
