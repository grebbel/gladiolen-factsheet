Page numbers below are the printed page numbers ("Pagina x van 40").

## Key findings

- RIVM summarises two studies: the OBO exposure study (*Onderzoek Bestrijdingsmiddelen en Omwonenden*; Vermeulen et al., 2019), which measured exposure of residents near flower bulb fields, and a 2018 health survey of residents near agricultural land (Simões et al., 2018) (p. 11–12).
- Bulb fields were chosen as an example of intensive pesticide use with downward spraying. Fruit growing (sideways and upward spraying) was planned as a later phase (p. 11–12).
- Pesticide residues from the bulb fields were found in outdoor air near homes, in doormat dust and in house dust, and in the urine of adults and children living nearby. They were also found in the urine of people living more than 500 m from fields (p. 3 and 5, summaries).
- Concentrations in air and house dust were higher at homes within 250 m than at control homes more than 500 m away: on average about 10 times higher in outdoor air and 5 times higher in house dust, averaged over all pesticides (p. 24).
- Concentrations in air and dust were lower outside the spraying period than during it, for both groups (p. 24).
- Individual results varied widely and overlapped between groups. Example: pendimethalin in outdoor air during the spraying period ranged from about 0.2 to 120 ng/m³ near fields and about 0.01 to 40 ng/m³ in the control group (p. 24).
- No measurable drift reached residents during the fieldwork, because the wind was not blowing towards homes when spraying. In experiments, drift was still measurable at 50 m from the spray site and at 10 m height. Exposure with drift can be more than 10 times higher than without (p. 24).
- Urine: tebuconazole and chlorpropham were measurable in 63% and 82% of urine samples respectively, in both residents and controls. Chlorpropham was on average twice as high in residents as in controls; for tebuconazole there was no such difference. Carbendazim was sometimes found; asulam and prochloraz only occasionally (p. 25).
- Measured urine concentrations were higher than could be explained by environmental exposure in the models. RIVM states that sources such as food residues "probably" contribute a large part (p. 27).
- Growers and their families: outdoor air at growers' homes was roughly 2 times higher, and house dust about 10 times higher, than at other residents' homes. Urine levels did not clearly differ (p. 26).
- **Risk assessment (indicative):** no measured value in air or urine exceeded available risk limits. Maximum air concentrations were at least 10 times below the fixed concentration used in authorisation (1 or 15 µg/m³). For urine, 185 of 192 participants were below the levels of volunteers given a safe daily dose; 7 were above that but below what would be expected from a safe short-term dose (pp. 29–30).
- RIVM stresses this is "geen eindoordeel" (not a final judgement): no worst-case exposure was measured, and only a selection of pesticides in one crop was studied (p. 30).
- The 2018 health survey found no health problems linked to living near bulb cultivation. It did find an association between maize and death from respiratory diseases, and some less clear observations for other crops. RIVM says the distance categories used were probably not able to distinguish differences, and recommends re-analysis (pp. 31–32).
- RIVM concludes that the current authorisation model (EFSA) does not underestimate exposure for the pesticides studied, but should add house dust as an exposure route and consider combined exposure to multiple pesticides (pp. 33–34; p. 36–37).
- Recommendations include: a research programme on health effects including vulnerable groups (children under 16 weeks), autism and cognitive development; national rules on minimum spray distance to homes and drift-reducing techniques; a knowledge platform; and reduced pesticide use (pp. 35–38).

## Figures and data

- Rural population within 250 m of an agricultural plot: about 30%; 18% if grassland is excluded (p. 11).
- At the time of fieldwork, 30 pesticides were authorised for tulip and lily cultivation (p. 20). (Gladiolus is not mentioned.)
- OBO participants: 164 residents within 250 m (125 adults, 39 children; 16 adults and 10 children from growers' families) in 80 homes; 28 controls in 16 homes; 40 growers; 9 central bulb fields and 127 other fields, of which 90% bulbs (p. 18, Table 1).
- 14 spray events on central fields; 1,048 environmental samples and 1,102 urine samples analysed (p. 19, Table 2).
- 40 pesticides found in outdoor and indoor air; 15 had been used on the bulb fields during the measuring period, 6 on nearby fields; the rest were not used on the studied fields in that period (p. 23).
- Garden soil: pendimethalin, prochloraz and pyraclostrobin 5 to 10 times higher than in the control group (p. 26).
- Ctgb has carried out a risk assessment for residents since early 2014; reassessed a selection of fruit and bulb pesticides in 2015 (no changes to authorisations); has used the EFSA model since early 2016 (p. 32).

## Methods and limitations

- Summary report; detailed methods are in Vermeulen et al. (2019) and Simões et al. (2018), which are not in `sources/`.
- Mineral oil, mancozeb and glyphosate were not measured because they need a different analytical method (p. 20).
- Not checked whether growers followed rules on dose, equipment and weather, or whether residents behaved differently during measurements (p. 20).
- Conditions were probably neither best-case nor worst-case (p. 25).

## Quality flags

- Not gladiolus-specific: bulb fields in general; the authorised-substances figure refers to tulips and lilies.
- Government institute report; secondary summary of studies not held in `sources/`.
- Fieldwork 2016–2017.
- Clear separation of detection (residues found) from risk (limits not exceeded), but RIVM itself says the risk picture is incomplete.
- **Differing figures across sources:** S4 gives about 30% of the *rural population* within 250 m of an agricultural plot (18% without grassland), while S2 and S3 give about 27% of *all homes* within 250 m of a cultivated field. These are different measures, not necessarily in conflict.
- **Differing urine figures:** S4 reports tebuconazole in 63% of urine samples (all participants), while S2 cites OBO as 61% for residents near bulb fields during the 2017 application period. S2 cites 97% (residents) and 67% (controls) for the chlorpropham metabolite; S4 reports 82% overall. These may be different subsets of the same data.
