Source ID in [[source-overview]]: S8. Written in Dutch. Dated 14 February 2026 (p. 2). PAN-NL is an advocacy organisation; the report contains explicit policy demands and consumer advice.

**Summary for this project:** **none of the 17 bouquets contained gladioli** (Annex 1, p. 22: roses, tulips, lilies, chrysanthemums, gerbera, lisianthus, alstroemeria and others). The report is therefore not evidence on residues on gladioli. It is relevant as context on residues on cut flowers in general, the absence of legal residue limits for ornamentals, and inspection results in Dutch cut-flower growing.

## Key findings

- In January 2026 PAN-NL bought 17 samples: 5 bunches of roses, 8 mixed bouquets and 4 bunches of tulips, from two supermarket chains, two local florists, an online retailer, a hospital, and the Nationale Tulpendag in Amsterdam (p. 3; p. 9).
- Residues were found in all samples (p. 9).
- Mixed bouquets and roses (13 samples): 87 different substances in total, including 8 breakdown products (metabolites); 57% insecticides, 43% fungicides (p. 9).
- Of the 79 active substances found (excluding metabolites), 22 (28%) are banned in the EU and the Netherlands as plant protection products (p. 9; p. 12, Table 2). 12 of the 13 mixed bouquets and rose bunches contained 1 to 13 EU-banned substances (p. 9).
- Tulips (4 samples, grown in the Netherlands): 9 different substances in total, 2 to 6 per bunch; average 0.21 mg/kg; no substances banned in the Netherlands for tulip growing (p. 10).
- PAN-NL classifies 51% of the substances found as highly hazardous pesticides (HHP, PAN International list), 19% as PFAS pesticides, and 14% as EU candidates for substitution; overall 78% are described as "een groot risico" (a high risk) for people and/or nature (p. 12, Table 2). **These are hazard classifications, not measured risk**: the report does not assess exposure of people handling the flowers.
- There are no legal maximum residue limits (MRLs) for pesticides on cut flowers, ornamental plants or flower bulbs, because they are not intended for consumption (p. 8; p. 10).
- The MPS certification scheme allows growers outside the EU to use EU-banned pesticides unless they are on the MPS banned list; 17 of the 21 EU-banned pesticides found (excluding avermectin) are allowed within MPS for non-EU countries (p. 5; p. 16).
- PAN-NL cites a 2026 BuRO advice (NVWA's Office for Risk Assessment and Research): imported cut flowers from outside the EU contain residues that may pose health and environmental risks; BuRO advises regulation and correct use of protective gloves by florists, auction staff and inspectors (pp. 19–20). The BuRO report itself is not in `sources/`.
- PAN-NL cites NVWA inspection results for cut flowers under glass, 2024–2025: 71 random inspections; only 39% of growers complied with plant protection rules; violations at 43 of 71 firms; 7 growers (10%) used products not authorised for the crop. Crops inspected included chrysanthemum, freesia, hydrangea and rose (pp. 20–21). The NVWA report itself is not in `sources/`.

## Figures and data

- Mixed bouquets: average 22.5 residues (range 12–35); average 4.8 mg/kg; 1 to 9 EU-banned substances (p. 9; p. 10, Table 1).
- Roses: average 17.4 residues (range 7–26); highest total 65.8 mg/kg (p. 9; p. 10, Table 1).
- Tulips: average 3.3 residues (range 2–6); highest 0.35 mg/kg (p. 10, Table 1).
- Laboratory: TLR International Laboratories, Ridderkerk; screened for 800 pesticides; limit of quantification 0.010 mg/kg; measurement uncertainty ±50% (p. 9).
- Comparison with earlier PAN-NL surveys: share of EU-banned substances among all substances found 30% (2022), 34% (2025), 28% (2026). PAN-NL states the number of samples and years is too low for trend analysis (p. 17, Table 4).
- Average pesticide use in the Netherlands in 2024, citing CBS: 5.0 kg/ha for all agricultural land; 8.6 kg/ha for floriculture crops ("bloemkwekerijgewassen"); 75.2 kg/ha for roses under glass (39.0 kg/ha in 2020) (p. 8; p. 21).
- More than 60% of world trade in flowers and plants passes through Dutch flower auctions; main supplying countries named include Ecuador, Colombia, Ethiopia and Kenya (p. 8; p. 11).
- EU-banned substances found (p. 15, Table 3) include asulam, carbendazim, prochloraz and imidacloprid, which other notes link to flower bulb growing. Which flowers in the bouquets carried them is not stated.

## Methods and limitations

- Small convenience sample (17), single time point, bought in Assen, Groningen, Utrecht, Amsterdam and online (Annex 1, p. 22).
- Flowers, stems and filler greenery analysed together, so residues cannot be linked to a flower species (p. 9).
- Origin of most bouquets unknown (for example p. 14 on avermectin).
- Hazard classifications rely on PAN International lists and the PPDB database (p. 2, disclaimer; p. 15).
- Annex tables of results per substance (pp. 23–27) are images only and were not extracted.

## Quality flags

- Advocacy organisation (NGO); not peer-reviewed. Claims should be checked against primary sources (BuRO, NVWA, CBS) where possible.
- Not gladiolus-specific: no gladioli sampled.
- Detection of residues, not harm; hazard labels are not risk assessments.
- **Internal inconsistencies:** the report gives 22 EU-banned substances (pp. 9, 12) and also 21 (pp. 5, 16; 21 when avermectin is excluded). The Tuinland mixed bouquet is given as 10.74 mg/kg in Table 1 (p. 10) but 19.7 mg/kg on p. 18. On p. 16 a list labelled "5 pesticides" names six substances.
