**Flower bulbs in general:** conclusions are based mainly on bulb farms. Crops on the sampled farms are not named; gladiolus, iris, crocus and freesia are not mentioned.

## Key findings

- Aim: compare the contribution of farmyard run-off (*erfafspoeling*) with spray drift from the field as causes of exceedances of surface-water standards (p. 4; p. 8).
- **Spray drift appears to be the most important factor in the number of exceedances of water-quality standards, probably in all agricultural sectors** (p. 5; p. 26).
- If a substance is used **only as a bulb dip**, farmyard emissions appear to be the main route for that substance. If it may be used both as a dip and as a field spray, drift appears more important because of the much larger length of ditches affected (p. 5; p. 26).
- Farmyard emissions can cause high concentration peaks. On farms with insufficient care during bulb disinfection, farmyard emissions can be an important route for these substances; where farmyard emissions are minimised, drift is more important (p. 5; p. 26).
- Substances registered as bulb dips (pyraclostrobin, imidacloprid, kresoxim-methyl, prochloraz, boscalid) gave the highest estimated maximum concentrations in the farmyard ditch: 10–100 µg/l (p. 23; p. 25).
- For the herbicides S-metolachlor, chloridazon and pendimethalin, estimated concentrations in a field ditch after spraying were at least 10 times higher than the estimated median in a farmyard ditch (p. 5; p. 23).
- For most selected substances, the estimated field-ditch concentration after spraying with 0.2% drift was above the MTR water-quality standard, while most met the authorisation criterion, except imidacloprid (p. 22).
- In "Schoon erf, schone sloot" projects, after growers took measures, concentrations in farmyard pits fell by 75% (Flevoland) and 68% (Noord-Holland) (p. 25; personal communication cited).
- Carbendazim and thiophanate-methyl were excluded from the comparison because their presence reflects historical load, not current use. Pirimiphos-methyl has not been authorised for bulbs since April 2014, only for grain storage (p. 18).
- The study does not say how often farmyard emissions occur, and does not translate results to regional waters or drinking-water intake points (p. 8).

## Methods and limitations

- Existing datasets from earlier farmyard-awareness projects; only detected values, no detection limits; farm locations unknown to the researchers (p. 9).
- Farmyard-ditch concentrations estimated with a worst-case calculation for 10 mm rain; field-ditch concentrations calculated from standard drift (0.2%, 90% drift reduction) on a standard Ctgb ditch (pp. 18; 21).
- Too few data for arable, horticulture and tree nurseries; conclusions focus on bulbs (p. 5; p. 17).

## Quality flags

- Recent (2022), multi-institute (WUR, CLM, RIVM), with government and sector oversight: moderate to strong for bulbs in general.
- Calculated (modelled) concentrations, not measured in-stream; worst-case assumptions.
- Not Iridaceae-specific.
- **Conflicts with earlier sources:** [[de-werd-2012-emissieroutes-water-abc]], [[lmb-2009-voortgangsrapportage]] and [[bisschops-2020-flow-schuimen-erfemissie]] identify the farmyard as the main source of exceedances in bulb growing. S19 finds drift is probably the main factor overall, with the farmyard dominant only for dip-only substances. The difference may partly reflect improved farmyard practices since 2007 and a different method (S19 itself cites the 2012 view on p. 8).
- An estimated concentration above a water-quality standard is a risk indicator, not observed ecological harm.
