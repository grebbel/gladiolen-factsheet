What the evidence shows about pesticide use, the environment and health.

*Last updated: 2 November 2026. Draft for review.*

## 1. Summary

Gladiolus is a small and shrinking crop in the Netherlands. About 660 hectares of gladiolus bulbs were grown in 2026, compared with about 1,640 hectares in 2000 [1]. Official statistics show that pesticide use on gladiolus fell sharply, from about 42 kilograms of active substance per hectare in 2012 to about 8 kilograms in 2024 [2]. These figures probably do not include soil disinfection before planting, which has been common in gladiolus growing [2][3]. Most of what is known about effects on water, nature and people comes from studies of flower bulbs in general, not gladiolus. Those studies show that pesticides from bulb fields reach nearby homes, ditches and people's bodies. For the substances studied, measured levels near homes stayed below health limits, but researchers say the picture is incomplete [4][5]. Key gaps remain, especially on workers' health, effects on nature, and which products are currently allowed on gladiolus.

## 2. Key findings

1. **Gladiolus growing has shrunk by about 60% since 2000**, to about 660 hectares of bulbs in 2026 and about 120–170 hectares grown for cut flowers [1]. *Evidence: strong.*
2. **Pesticide use per hectare on gladiolus fell by about 80% between 2012 and 2024**, mainly because fewer fungicides were used [2]. *Evidence: strong.* Soil disinfection is probably not included in these figures [2][3]. *Evidence: limited.*
3. **Pesticides are still used on almost all gladiolus fields** (99.6% of the area in 2024), and the number of different active substances used rose from 23 to 34 [2]. *Evidence: strong.*
4. **The Netherlands exports far more gladiolus corms than it imports**: about 460–760 million corms exported per year, against 2–33 million imported [6]. *Evidence: strong.*
5. **Pesticides from bulb fields reach nearby homes and residents.** Near bulb fields, levels in outdoor air were on average about 10 times higher, and in house dust about 5 times higher, than at homes far from fields [4]. *Evidence: strong for detection; this is not gladiolus-specific.*
6. **For the substances studied, levels did not exceed health limits**, but no worst case was measured and combined exposure to several substances was not assessed [4]. *Evidence: moderate.*
7. **Pesticides from bulb growing exceed water-quality standards in ditches.** Experts disagree whether the farmyard or spray drift from the field is the main route [3][5][7]. *Evidence: conflicting.*

## 3. Gladiolus and related crops in the Netherlands

### How much gladiolus is grown

Gladiolus is grown from corms: underground storage organs similar to bulbs. Growers produce corms for sale and also grow gladioli as cut flowers.

- In 2000 about 1,643 hectares of gladiolus corms were grown. By 2010 this was about 1,118 hectares, by 2020 about 812, and in 2026 about 662 hectares (provisional figure) [1].
- The number of farms growing gladiolus corms fell from 320 to 90 over the same period [1].
- About 118–169 hectares per year were grown as open-field cut flowers in 2017–2026 [1].
- The total area of all flower bulbs grew over the same period. Gladiolus fell from about 7% to about 2% of the Dutch bulb area [1].

*Evidence: strong (official statistics).*

Different statistics give different areas. The national agricultural census gives higher figures than the bulb inspection service, because it also counts field edges and records the area at the farm's postal address [1][3][8]. For the 1990s, two reports from the bulb sector give very different figures (about 900 and about 1,900 hectares for 1995) [3][8]. *Evidence for 1995: conflicting.*

### Where it is grown

Unlike tulips and hyacinths, which are concentrated in a few regions, gladiolus is grown across the country [9]. In a recent year (not specified by the source), the largest share of the gladiolus area was in the CBS agricultural region Zuidelijk Veehouderijgebied (27%), followed by Veenkoloniën en Oldambt (13%), Westelijk Holland (12%) and the Zuidwestelijk Akkerbouwgebied (12%) [9]. In the 2000s, reports placed gladiolus mainly in Limburg, Noord-Brabant, the south-west and the IJsselmeerpolders [3][8]. *Evidence: moderate.*

### Related crops in the iris family

Gladiolus belongs to the iris family (*Iridaceae*). Because gladiolus-specific data are scarce, this factsheet also uses data on related crops, clearly labelled.

- **Iris** (open field): about 675 hectares in 2000, about 180 hectares in 2026 [1].
- **Crocus** (open field): about 628 hectares in 2000, about 314 hectares in 2026 [1].
- **Freesia** (grown under glass for cut flowers): about 221 hectares in 2000, about 50 hectares in 2025 [1].

*Evidence: strong.*

### Imports and exports

- **Gladiolus corms:** in 2015–2025 the Netherlands imported 2–33 million corms per year, mainly from outside the EU, and exported 464–755 million per year, worth €26–38 million [6].
- **Cut gladioli:** imports were 0–15 million stems per year, almost all from other EU countries. Exports were 23–105 million stems per year [6].

*Evidence: strong for the order of magnitude.* The trade figures include goods passing through the Netherlands, 2021 is missing, and recent years are provisional [6]. Countries of origin and trade in iris, crocus and freesia were not found in the sources.

## 4. Pesticide use

### Key terms

- **Active substance**: the ingredient in a pesticide that acts against the pest, disease or weed.
- **Fungicides** act against fungal diseases, **herbicides** against weeds, and **insecticides** against insects.
- **Soil disinfection**: treating the soil before planting to kill nematodes (tiny worms) and other soil pests.

### How much is used on gladiolus

Statistics Netherlands (CBS) surveyed pesticide use on gladiolus in 2012, 2016, 2020 and 2024 [2].

| Year | Area treated (ha) | Total used (kg) | kg per hectare |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2012 | 1,113 | 46,675 | 41.9 |
| 2016 | 929 | 33,151 | 35.7 |
| 2020 | 812 | 15,722 | 19.4 |
| 2024 (provisional) | 695 | 5,756 | 8.2 |

*Text alternative: pesticide use on gladiolus fell from 41.9 to 8.2 kilograms of active substance per hectare between 2012 and 2024.*

- Fungicides made up most of the use and fell the most, from 30.4 to 3.6 kg per hectare. Herbicides fell from 8.8 to 3.9, and insecticides from 2.6 to 0.4 kg per hectare [2].
- Pesticides were used on 99.6–100% of the gladiolus area in every survey year [2].
- In 2020 gladiolus used less per hectare than lily bulbs (113.7 kg) and tulips (25.8 kg), and more than hyacinths (17.0 kg). The average for all crops was 7.0 kg [2].

*Evidence: strong.*

**Two cautions.** First, the CBS figures probably leave out soil disinfection: CBS records no use of the main soil disinfectant, metam-sodium, on any crop [2]. A 2007 sector report described soil disinfection before planting gladiolus, mainly in Limburg and Noord-Brabant, after other products against soil pests disappeared [3]. In 2007 the bulb sector as a whole used on average 50 kg per hectare of metam-sodium on 3,457 hectares [3]. Current practice was not found in the sources. *Evidence: limited.* Second, less weight does not mean less harm. The statistics do not measure how toxic the substances are.

### Which substances are used

- In 2012 the substance used most by weight on gladiolus was the fungicide mancozeb (14,858 kg, on 68% of the area) [2]. Mancozeb has been banned since 2021 [10].
- In 2024 the largest were the fungicide folpet (1,017 kg) and the herbicides pendimethalin (882 kg, on 90% of the area), metamitron (652 kg) and glyphosate (615 kg), followed by the fungicide captan (368 kg) [2].
- The number of different active substances recorded on gladiolus rose from 23 in 2012 to 34 in 2024 [2].

*Evidence: strong.*

Around 2005, spraying in gladiolus was aimed mainly at the fungal disease *Botrytis gladioli* and at thrips (small insects), usually every week from about June into November [8]. *Evidence: limited.* A model study estimated that adjusting the spray to the size of the crop could cut use by about 35% [8]. *Evidence: limited; not tested in practice.*

Several substances once used in bulb growing are no longer allowed, including carbendazim for bulbs (2003), aldicarb (2007), pirimiphos-methyl for bulbs (2014), mancozeb (2021), thiophanate-methyl (2023) and oxamyl (2023) [3][5][10]. *Evidence: moderate.*

### Related crops

- **Iris:** about 27–35 kg per hectare in 1995–2000, read from a chart [8]. Current figures were not found in the sources. *Evidence: limited.*
- **Freesia (under glass):** thrips is hard to control and few chemical products are available. In one greenhouse in 2009, biological control failed and broad-spectrum insecticides were used [11]. *Evidence: limited.*

### Residues on flowers

No study in the sources tested gladioli for residues. A 2026 survey by an environmental organisation found residues on all 17 bouquets it tested, but none contained gladioli [12]. There are no legal residue limits for cut flowers or bulbs [12].

## 5. Environmental impacts

### Pesticides in surface water

In bulb-growing areas, 13–19% of water samples in 2001–2007 contained one of four substances of concern above the maximum permissible risk level (MTR, a water-quality standard). Water quality had improved since 1995, but progress had stalled [3]. *Evidence: moderate; data up to 2007.*

A level above a water standard means there is a risk to water life. It does not by itself show that harm has occurred.

### How pesticides reach water: experts disagree

- Earlier reports name the **farmyard** as the main source in bulb growing: run-off after bulbs are disinfected or crates are washed, and water from storage rooms [3][7][13].
- A 2022 study by WUR, CLM and RIVM concludes that **spray drift** from the field is probably the main cause of exceeded standards. The farmyard is the main route only for substances used solely to treat bulbs [5].
- After growers took measures, levels in farmyard collection pits fell by 68–75% [5].

*Evidence: conflicting.* The difference may partly reflect better farmyard practice over time.

The 2022 study calculated that one spray with standard drift can give ditch levels above water standards for several bulb substances. For pendimethalin, the estimate was 1.52 micrograms per litre, against a standard of 0.018 [5]. Pendimethalin was one of the most-used substances on gladiolus in 2024 [2]. *Evidence: moderate; calculated, not measured, and not specific to gladiolus.*

Carbendazim stayed in the environment after it was withdrawn for bulbs, because its replacement in bulb treatment breaks down into carbendazim. It was found in surface water up to 2007 and in almost all indoor dust samples near bulb fields in 2016–2017 [3][14]. *Evidence: moderate.*

### What is not known

The sources contain no measurements of effects on soil life, bees and other pollinators, birds or water life near gladiolus or other iris-family crops. No recent water-quality data for gladiolus areas were found.

## 6. Health impacts

### Hazard and risk

- **Hazard** is what a substance can do in principle, for example harm the nervous system.
- **Risk** is how likely harm is, given how much people are actually exposed.
- Finding a substance in air, dust or urine shows **exposure**. It does not by itself show **harm**.

### People living near bulb fields

The largest Dutch study of residents near bulb fields measured pesticides in and around 96 homes in 2016–2017 [4][14]. The crops on the fields are not named, so the results apply to flower bulbs in general.

- Pesticides from the fields were found in outdoor air, doormat dust and house dust, and in the urine of adults and children living nearby [4].
- Within 250 metres of fields, outdoor air levels were on average about 10 times higher, and house dust levels about 5 times higher, than at homes more than 500 metres away [4].
- Pesticides were also found in the urine of people living far from fields. Food probably contributes a large part of this [4].
- Growers and their families had higher levels at home: about 10 times more in house dust and about 2 times more in outdoor air than other residents [4].
- A 2020 wastewater study found higher levels of two pesticide breakdown products in one of two bulb-growing towns [15].

*Evidence: strong for exposure (detection).*

For the substances studied, levels in air and urine did not exceed available health limits [4]. RIVM, the national public health institute, stresses that this is not a final judgement: no worst case was measured, only some substances in one crop group were studied, and the combined effect of several substances was not assessed [4]. *Evidence: moderate.*

### Health outcomes

A study of 3.1 million Dutch residents found no link between living near flower bulbs and causes of death. Exposure was estimated from the crop area around homes in 1995–2003, not measured [16]. A 2018 health survey also found no health problems linked to bulb growing, but RIVM says its distance categories were probably too coarse to show differences [4]. *Evidence: limited. Finding no link is not proof of no harm.*

### Workers

No study in the sources measured the exposure or health of gladiolus or freesia workers. A trade article notes that newer bulb-treatment methods reduce workers' contact with the products [13]. *Evidence: limited.*

## 7. Regulation and oversight

- Pesticides are approved under EU rules (Regulation (EC) 1107/2009). In the Netherlands the Ctgb, the national authorisation board, decides which products are allowed and how they may be used [4][17].
- Since 2014 the Ctgb has assessed risks for residents, and since 2016 it has used the European (EFSA) method [4]. RIVM found this method did not underestimate exposure for the substances studied, but recommended adding house dust and combined exposure, and national rules on spraying distance and drift reduction [4]. Whether this has happened was not found in the sources.
- EU rules require pesticide use to be banned or minimised in places used by the public or vulnerable groups, such as schools and playgrounds [17].
- A current list of products allowed on gladiolus, iris, crocus or freesia was not found in the sources.

*Evidence: strong for the EU and national framework.*

### The role of municipalities is disputed

- Environmental organisations and law firms argue that municipalities may set spray-free zones or ban certain crops, and recommend zones of 250 metres [17].
- The municipality of Berg en Dal (2026) accepts it has these powers, but sees regulation mainly as a national task and has no plans for local rules. It keeps 50 metres between new homes and farmland [18].

*Evidence: conflicting (differing legal and policy views).*

### Compliance

- In 2007, checks in the bulb sector found that the products reported did not match the products used at 62.5% of farms checked. Water boards found violations at 8–40% of farms they inspected [3].
- In 2024–2025, only 39% of 71 inspected cut-flower growers under glass complied with the rules, according to the national inspectorate (NVWA) as cited by an environmental organisation [12].
- Berg en Dal does not check farmers' pesticide records; the NVWA is the main supervisor [18].

*Evidence: limited (old data, or not specific to bulbs or gladiolus).*

## 8. Evidence gaps and uncertainties

**Not found in the sources:**

1. Which products are currently allowed on gladiolus, iris, crocus and freesia.
2. How much soil and corm disinfection is used in gladiolus growing today.
3. Current pesticide use on iris, crocus and freesia.
4. Where imported gladiolus corms and flowers come from.
5. Pesticide residues on cut gladioli or traded corms.
6. Measured effects on soil, water life, pollinators and birds near gladiolus fields.
7. Exposure and health of gladiolus and freesia workers.
8. Health studies that look at gladiolus separately, and the combined effect of several pesticides.
9. Recent inspection results for bulb or gladiolus growing.

**Main uncertainties:**

- Evidence on the environment and health comes from flower bulbs in general. The link to gladiolus rests on shared substances and practices.
- The official use figures probably leave out soil disinfection.
- Sources disagree on the main route by which pesticides reach water, and on the role of municipalities.
- Figures from the 1990s differ between sources, and some were read from charts.

## 10. About this factsheet

**What it is.** An independent personal investigation into pesticide use in gladiolus growing in the Netherlands and its effects on the environment and health. It has no institutional affiliation.

**Method.** Sources were collected from 2010 onwards where possible, with older sources used for historical context. Each source was summarised in a note, and the findings were combined in a synthesis. Every statement on this page is based on the numbered sources. Where something was not found, the page says so.

**Evidence labels.**

- *Strong*: several independent sources agree, or official statistics or a large, well-designed study.
- *Moderate*: one good-quality source, or several that share limitations.
- *Limited*: a single source with clear limitations, such as old data, a small sample, a trade or advocacy source, or values read from a chart.
- *Conflicting*: sources disagree; both views are given.

**Limitations.** Little research is specific to gladiolus. Most evidence on the environment and health is about flower bulbs in general. Some sources are from environmental organisations or trade press; their claims have been checked against official sources where possible.
